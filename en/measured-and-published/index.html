E-101

We measured our own agent, and published the bad number

Draft article: not published or announced; it waits for the guard's and the owner's verdict.

We gave ARCY nineteen real tasks and paid for every run. Eleven passed, and the owner judged that no output was good enough to show a client; this is that record, and what changed the next day.

ARCY is an AI agent for architecture, design, engineering and urban planning. It was born in Saudi Arabia and is built for anyone, anywhere. This blog begins where the work stands today, not where we would like it to stand.

What we measured

On 23 November 2026 we ran the agent on 19 tasks of the kind a person brings to an architect: a figure from a design guideline with its page, a plot from its sketch, two land listings compared, a site of four plots, a two-storey house from a short request, its render, quantities and daylight, a client board, a walkthrough film. Every task ran once, on one model, through the same agent a person would use.

The result: 11 of 19 tasks passed, which is 58%. 46 of 57 criteria were met, and 25 of 205 steps failed.

The areas were not equal. The guideline lookup and the study of a site's surroundings passed 2 of 2. Site, plot, property, rules and reviews passed 2 of 8. Building, render, quantities, daylight, options, facade, board, picture and film passed 7 of 9.

What a pass means, and what it does not

A pass in this suite says that a capability was used correctly and the task's criteria were met. It does not say the output is good.

So the owner of ARCY looked at every output. The scale runs from 0 to 4, and 3 is what a large consultancy would show a client. The verdict: no output is above 1 of 4. The record says why: the drawings carry two overall dimensions and no grids, the buildings are boxes on empty ground, the boards are mostly empty.

A model reviewer had first put most outputs at 2. That score was too generous and it is not the record. In our plan, the owner's eye is the judge of quality, and the target is 3 or above.

So there are two numbers, read together: eleven of nineteen is how often the agent did the task, one of four how good the result was. Publishing the first alone would mislead.

Eleven of nineteen is how often the agent did the task. One of four is how good the result was.

Why we publish it

Our working agreement has a line for this: measured, not claimed, with failed checks and the limits of a verification kept in the record. Our plan says it another way: every claim has a number, and what was not measured is said to be not measured.

A weak number is also useful: it is the "before" that every later "after" is read against, on the same tasks.

We show no picture of these outputs. Our rule is that an ARCY output appears only after the owner scores it 3 of 4 or above, and none has reached that yet.

The limits of this number

  • One run per task. A task that passed once may fail on a second run, and the reverse.
  • One model. Other models were not part of this measurement.
  • Not everything was measured. Research, memory, project records, capture, video reading, delegation and the tool maker were left out.
  • We stopped at nineteen. The owner ruled a planned third batch unnecessary: the problems were already clear.
  • Two of our own findings were wrong. We wrote that Arabic file names were lost; they are kept, and we had read the wrong name. We wrote that the agent goes around its specialised engines; that held for one task only. Both corrections are on record.

What changed the next day

On 24 November we read the session records of the failed tasks, step by step, looking for causes. Five fixes followed. We reran the four tasks they touch: 3 of 4 pass now, against 0 of 4 before.

  • The guideline question: failed in 33 steps and 104 seconds before; passes in 1 step and 11 seconds after.
  • The plot from its sketch: 7 steps before, 5 of them failed; 2 steps after, none failed.
  • The two typed land listings: none was kept before; both are kept after.
  • The site of four plots still fails: the right guide is read now, but the site model is still not made.

Four tasks, one run each, give a direction and no more; the statistical test on them returns p = 0.25. One of the three passes also needed its scorer corrected: the scorer read a recorded value the wrong way, so the task could not have passed whatever the agent did.

And the owner's verdict on the nineteen outputs stands. A pass is still only a pass.

A pass is still only a pass.

Some things were not fixed, and the record says why. A review of a clause in a person's own document cannot yet be re-checked by measurement. The facade task asked for sun-breakers, which our facade engine does not make; the task's criterion was wrong for the request, and shading devices are a gap in the engine. Two more failed tasks were not touched.

What we do next

The plan holds four cycles of repair for the outputs: the agent using what it has, professional drawings and boards, the design itself, and honest rendering in context. Each cycle is a fix, a before-and-after measurement on the same tasks, and then the owner's verdict on the output.

The phase ends when a complete villa journey (plans, elevation, section, picture and board) gets 3 from the owner on every output. A later phase answers the "one run" limit: each of the ten most common requests is to succeed 9 of 10 times, with at least three runs.

The plan also says that ARCY's numbers are published and every fix is measured before and after. Those figures belong in this record too, whether they move or not.
